# Worked example

A fictional user, to show the shape of a good evaluation.

## The profile (abridged)

> Mira is a backend developer with a full-time job. On the side she sells **StockSync**, a small B2B add-on that keeps inventory in sync between Shopify and a warehouse system: 14 paying shops, $29/month. She also writes a technical blog that brings in the add-on's leads.
>
> **Hard rules:** no paid acquisition until the add-on makes $1,000/month, because she won't fund growth from her salary. No personal posting on social media, which is about privacy, not effort.
>
> **Decision log** has an entry from March: *"Idea marketplaces and 'steal this idea' lists: not a source of ideas. We already have one product; the bottleneck is distribution, not ideas."*

## The input

> "Saw a thread: a meal-planning app makes $300k/month, according to an app-revenue tracker. They pay 18 creators to post near-identical TikToks three times a day, with a shocked face and a caption like *'8 years shopping at Walmart and I ONLY NOW found this???'*. Should we do this for StockSync?"

## The evaluation

**Verdict: TAKE A PIECE.** The creator farm is paid acquisition aimed at the wrong buyer, so it fails two ways. The caption trick transfers to the blog for free.

**What it is:** a consumer subscription app that grows through volume. Many hired creators post many cheap videos, a few go viral, and those pay for the rest.

**Source and evidence:** the $300k is an **estimate** from a third-party tracker, not revenue. The thread shows one winner. The many apps that ran the same playbook and burned their budget aren't in it. That points to a real mechanism with survivorship-shaped evidence.

**Why:**
- **It's paid acquisition.** 18 creators × 3 videos a day plus view bonuses comes to roughly $10–30k a month. That breaks the "no paid acquisition before $1k/month" rule by an order of magnitude.
- **Wrong buyer.** The mechanic needs a mass-market product, a purchase decided in seconds, and a product whose use is itself the ad. StockSync is bought by shop owners after a trial, with a warehouse integration to set up. A shocked face doesn't sell that.
- The no-social-posting rule isn't what decides this: hired creators would post, not Mira. The money rule does.

**What we keep:** the caption's first frame selects its own audience, and the swappable detail ("Walmart") is the segment. For Mira's blog, write one article on syncing inventory, then publish 3–4 variants whose title swaps the platform: Shopify + ShipBob, Shopify + ShipHero, WooCommerce + ShipBob. One piece of writing, several self-selecting entry points, no new research.

**Next:** add a task: "Retitle the top inventory-sync post into three platform variants, then check which one gets search impressions after 4 weeks." Log entry: *"Paid creator farms: not before the $1k/month gate, and not for products bought after a trial. Self-selecting first-line captions: adopted for blog titles."*
